_"I build solutions for developers that replace toil with self-service and automation, and help enterprise organizations be more effective and efficient at delivering software."_

That's the cover-letter version, here's the interpretation:

## Sympathy for Developers

Software developers should be focused on creative work - building solutions that have no present-day equivalent, and with a sustained curiosity to improve on what has been done before.  "Flow" is a real thing and interruptions can be costly.  Touchpoints between a developer and their environment should be familiar and frictionless:

* Provisioning a development environment.
* Obtaining entitlements.
* Accessing technical documentation.
* Guidance for issue-tracking, branching strategy, coding style, and documentation.
* Building, validating, and promoting a change.

### Productivity Inhibitors
* A perpetually out-of-date catalog of approved tools and dependencies.
* Executing individual installation commands from one or more documentation sources.
* Identifying, requesting and waiting for required entitlement approvals.
* Resolving breaks in CI and higher environments that cannot be replicated locally.
* Manual execution of deployments.
* Support escalations.
* Sifting through technical documentation of varying consistency, quality, and relevance. 
* Attending meetings that have no collaborative aspect.

These challenges are compounded when the procedures differ between projects in the organization.

## Sympathy for Organizations
Organizations want the same thing as developers - for things to _just work_.

Organizations, especially larger ones, have their own concerns:
* **Licensing**:  A dependency that's "Not for Commercial Use" is a problem.
* **Cybersecurity**:  Not all bugs are equal.  Breaches can be terminal.
* **Regulatory**:  Governments and legal jurisdictions protect their interests with rules.  Not suggestions, rules.
* **Inventory**: Maintaining compliance with the above items.
* **Dependency Management**:  Need to deprecate a "minor" API, or patch a vulnerability?  See [Ripple effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripple_effect).
* **Technical Archeology**: Projects can reflect what "modern" architecture looked like on their "go live" day. Business acquisitions add another dimension of technical sprawl.
* **Shareholders and Customers**: They expect results, and have little sympathy for internal inefficiencies.

## Intractable != Impossible
Throughout my career I've been solving problems that arise when the needs of developers and organizations are in conflict.  Experience includes complementary perspectives:
* Developing [paved roads](https://thenewstack.io/paved-roads-golden-paths-guardrails-and-railroads/).
* Technical evangelism of paved roads.
* Leader of teams who struggled with bumpy pavement.

Every situation is different, and my approach adapts accordingly while maintaining two common themes:

### Culture of Collaboration
When organizations become siloed and things are going wrong, defensive walls can emerge with fingers pointing from above the parapet.  When the fingers are aligned towards me or my team, it's a prime opportunity to build trust through transparency and engagement.  When a team is struggling to provide a needed dependency, engage to build understanding and explore ways to help unblock.

### Code and Self-Service > Slide Decks and Spreadsheets
I encourage my team to communicate using prototypes and scripts, and to adopt or develop tools that reduce toil.

As a Director of a platform services team, I went hands-on on several occasions alongside my senior-level duties:
* Built a solution that freed my managers from reporting toil.
* Prototyped a platform control plane.  
* When my team inherited a project with a challenged history of stability, I got involved to help diagnose the root causes of intra-day outages.
  

## Current Project
Inspiration to develop the [Value Stream Simulator](https://github.com/bvett/value-stream-simulator) came from separate experiences in helping teams comply with senior-level mandates, such as code coverage minimums, continuous delivery, and cloud adoption. These taught me:

* Mandates (and hidden costs) can be avoided when the _paved road_ is also the path of least resistance.
* Unit test coverage can be gamed and is not an indicator of production stability.  
* A CD toolchain doesn't address the bottleneck affecting a project with a monthly release cadence.

The Value Stream Simulator aims to express how changes to the development ecosystem (practices, tooling, resourcing) affects delivered value by enabling data-driven comparisons between the cost of changing and the expected return.
